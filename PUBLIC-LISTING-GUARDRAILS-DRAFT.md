# Draft: How Bills Qualify for the DC Bills Tracker

**Status:** Draft for public review — not yet approved or published  
**Last updated:** September 8, 2026

The DC Bills Tracker is an advocacy tool, not a neutral index of every bill that
mentions Washington, DC. Its purpose is to identify congressional measures that
interfere with DC's ability to govern itself and to explain why they matter.

This page describes the guardrails used to decide what appears on the tracker,
how a bill is labeled, and where human judgment is required.

## The basic rule

A bill, resolution, or budget rider may be publicly listed as anti-DC only when
there is evidence that it does at least one of the following:

1. Interferes with how the District of Columbia is governed under Home Rule;
2. Interferes with how DC governs itself under Home Rule; or
3. Uses authority Congress would not have over DC if DC were a state—for
   example, overriding a local law, vetoing local legislation, or changing DC's
   boundaries without its consent.

A DC reference by itself is not enough. Neither sponsorship, party affiliation,
committee assignment, nor an automated relevance score determines whether a
bill is anti-DC.

## How a bill reaches the public tracker

Bills may be identified through trusted advocacy sources, the D.C. Statehood
Scorecard, news and community submissions, or automated Congress.gov searches.
Discovery is only a lead; it is not a classification.

An automatically discovered bill remains provisional and must not be presented
to the public as anti-DC until either:

- a human reviewer confirms that the bill meets the test above; or
- a documented rule, backed by authoritative bill text or an approved external
  classification, establishes that it meets the test.

If the evidence is unclear, the bill is flagged for human review. It is not
silently labeled `oppose`.

## Classification after review

Each reviewed bill receives two separate judgments:

### Our position on the bill

- **Oppose:** The bill harms DC's self-government or DC residents.
- **Routine:** Congress is performing an existing, recurring obligation over DC
  that a state would handle for itself. This is a structural cost of DC's status,
  but not a newly introduced restriction.
- **Support:** The bill expands self-government or benefits DC residents. Support
  bills are currently maintained separately and are not displayed on this
  oppose-focused tracker.

### How directly it targets DC

- **Direct:** Interfering with DC is a primary purpose of the measure.
- **Partial:** The measure has a broader purpose but also overrides or restricts
  DC's self-government.

These judgments are independent. For example, a bill can involve congressional
control over DC's laws while still having content that advocates support.

## What automation may and may not decide

Automation may:

- find bills that could be relevant;
- collect official titles, text, sponsors, actions, and status;
- suggest possible categories for human review; and
- calculate legislative momentum from documented activity.

Automation may not, by itself:

- decide that a bill is anti-DC;
- assign an advocacy position from a keyword, sponsor, or relevance score;
- turn a provisional suggestion into a public classification; or
- overwrite a reviewed classification without flagging the change.

## Priority is not severity

Priority describes how urgently a bill may require attention based on legislative
momentum, such as a hearing, markup, floor vote, or growing sponsorship. It does
not measure how harmful the bill is. A severe proposal can have little momentum,
and a narrower proposal can move quickly.

Partial attacks reach high priority only through demonstrated legislative
momentum, not merely because they were manually added or appear on an external
list.

## Public status sections

Every publicly listed measure must appear in exactly one current-status section.
Sections must be mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive: no bill should
appear twice, and no tracked bill should disappear because its status does not
fit a section.

At minimum, the status model must distinguish:

- pending in Congress;
- passed the House only;
- passed the Senate only;
- passed both chambers but not enacted; and
- enacted.

Section headings and explanatory copy must describe the actual status. A
Senate-only bill, a bill passed by both chambers, or an enacted law must not be
described simply as "Passed the House" or "headed to the Senate."

## Quality controls

Before public data changes are deployed:

- each bill must resolve to the intended official Congress.gov record;
- provisional items must remain visibly separated from reviewed classifications;
- classifications must satisfy the rules above;
- generated counts and statistics must agree with the underlying bill data;
- reviewed examples must not drift without a documented reason; and
- automated consistency checks must pass.

When the tracker differs from a trusted external source, the difference should
be documented rather than hidden.

## Corrections and accountability

Classification involves judgment, and mistakes are possible. Corrections should
identify what changed and why. Questions or challenges should be evaluated
against official bill text and these published guardrails, not against an
automated label alone.

## Questions for review before publication

1. Where should failed, withdrawn, superseded, and prior-Congress measures live:
   in separate current-status sections, an archive, or outside the active tracker?
2. How should simple and concurrent resolutions be categorized when their legal
   path does not follow the ordinary House–Senate–President sequence?
3. Is an approved external classification enough to publish a bill, or should
   every new bill receive an explicit RepresentDC human review first?
4. Should the public page use the term "anti-DC" for the `routine` category, or
   explain routine measures only as structural consequences of denied statehood?
