🚨 DoorDash-Backed Bill Just Passed Committee — On a Party-Line Vote

Yesterday, the House Oversight Committee voted 23–18 along party lines to advance H.R. 9720, the "D.C. Taxing Authority Review Act," out of committee. The bill's lead corporate backer isn't a think tank or a Hill staffer — it's DoorDash, retaliating against the D.C. Council's new 20-cent delivery fee.

Take action now: Delete the DoorDash app, cancel your account, and post a screenshot tagged #HR9720 / #DeleteDoorDash. Free DC has launched a flash campaign demanding DoorDash immediately withdraw its support for H.R. 9720 — this is the fastest way to make DoorDash feel the cost of attacking D.C. home rule.

Where the fee actually came from

Some background, via NOTUS's Martin Austermuhle: the D.C. Council approved the 20-cent delivery fee on July 7 during a difficult budget cycle, after Mayor Bowser proposed cutting hundreds of millions in social-program funding to close a $1.1 billion budget gap. Ward 1 Councilmember Brianne Nadeau, who proposed the fee, designed it to fund food-access programs and oversight of third-party delivery services — the money is projected to raise $6.6 million a year, funding things like a low-income Instacart membership pilot, grocery-delivery support for a Ward 8 nonprofit, and grants for new food businesses in underserved neighborhoods. DoorDash fought the fee before it passed — a petition, social media ads, a company-commissioned poll, even a mobile digital billboard outside the Wilson Building — and lost. H.R. 9720 is the company's next move after that loss.

Credit where it's due: Free DC and D.C. leaders showed up

Free DC didn't just react after the fact — they mobilized supporters to pack Wednesday's committee hearing room and ran a call campaign urging committee members to "stay out of DC's budget" before the vote even happened. Once DoorDash's support for the bill came to light, Free DC moved fast, posting on Instagram Wednesday night to demand DoorDash's "immediate withdrawal of your support for #HR9720," warning: "DC will be deleting our accounts on your platform until we hear from you" — reported by WUSA9 and DC News Now.

It's not just advocates — sitting D.C. officials are acting too. Ward 5 Councilmember Zachary Parker, who supported the delivery fee, told WUSA9 he's personally deleted the app: "I am joining my neighbors in deleting the app. I will no longer use it as a service personally." He didn't mince words about DoorDash's position: "DoorDash actively lobbying in support of Congress making decisions on behalf of District residents is antithetical to home rule. You can't have it both ways. You can't say I support home rule, yet I am going to actively lobby Congress against the District."

Council Chair Phil Mendelson made the institutional case in his own letter to Chairman Comer, writing that the bill "would straightjacket the District because it would paralyze our tax code," since it "applies to both tax increases AND decreases" and would "also freeze our General License Law." We could not confirm any co-signers on Mendelson's letter — it appears to be his alone, not a joint Council letter. But he wasn't the only Council voice: At-Large Councilmember Robert White issued his own statement the day before the markup, warning the bill "would let a Congress that can't even keep its own government open decide whether D.C. can fund its own police, schools, and services," and noting the District has "balanced our budget more than 30 years running." D.C.'s congressional delegate, Eleanor Holmes Norton, also released opposition remarks ahead of the markup.

This is what it looks like when local advocacy, elected officials, and D.C.'s institutional leadership all turn a corporate ally against the District into a liability.

What H.R. 9720 actually does

The bill would require an affirmative resolution of approval from Congress before any D.C. action under Title 47 of the D.C. Code can take effect — that's taxation, licensing, permits, assessments, and fees, not just delivery fees. It would apply to both tax increases and decreases, and would freeze D.C.'s General License Law. Council Chair Phil Mendelson has warned it would "straightjacket" the District and "paralyze" its tax code entirely, and could trigger a bond rating downgrade.

Here's the concrete mechanism, per WUSA9's reporting: today, a D.C. Council tax or fee change takes effect automatically unless Congress objects within a 30-day review period. H.R. 9720 replaces that with a 60-day window in which Congress must affirmatively approve the change — if it doesn't, the measure dies, even if the Council already passed it.

DoorDash's own words don't add up

In a letter entered into the official record by Chairman Comer, DoorDash's head of policy, John Horton, described the bill far more narrowly than it actually is:

  • Scope: Horton's letter claims the bill only requires approval "before the District of Columbia can impose or increase a tax or fee." In reality, it covers all Title 47 actions — including tax and fee decreases and D.C.'s general licensing law — a much broader freeze on D.C.'s ability to govern its own finances than DoorDash admitted to.
  • "Normal" review: Horton frames the bill as just adding the same kind of "affirmative, time-limited congressional review" already used for other D.C. legislation — as if this were routine. It isn't. Under current Home Rule law, Congress reviews D.C. legislation passively — a bill becomes law unless Congress affirmatively votes to disapprove it. H.R. 9720 flips that: nothing takes effect unless Congress affirmatively approves it first. That's a fundamental, first-of-its-kind change to how D.C. self-governance works — not a "reasonable check" already in common use, as AFSCME's opposition letter makes clear.
  • "We support Home Rule": In a separate statement given directly to WUSA9, Horton claimed "we have always supported Home Rule for D.C." — while DoorDash simultaneously lobbies Congress to strip the D.C. Council's ability to enact tax and fee policy without Washington's sign-off. Councilmember Parker's response: "You can't have it both ways."

Also opposing the bill

AFSCME (1.4 million members) warned the bill "endangers public services in D.C." and would block a proposed Business Activity Tax that could raise $500 million for public services. The League of Women Voters called it "the latest in a long line of attacks on DC's democracy." D.C. Shadow Senator Ankit Jain joined local residents packing Wednesday's markup in opposition, and even on the committee dais, Rep. Melanie Stansbury (D-N.Mex.) told Chairman Comer: "I think it's obvious this bill was not written by the people of D.C."
